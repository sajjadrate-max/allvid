Turning a video into audio for podcasts and lectures

Published October 25, 2026 · 3 min read

A lot of video is really about the words. A lecture with static slides, a recorded sermon, an interview or a podcast that is also posted as a video can be just as useful as audio, and audio is far smaller, easier on data and perfect for listening while you walk, drive or cook. Here is how to make the most of audio-only saving, for content you own or are allowed to keep.

When audio is the better choice

  • The picture does not add much. Talking heads, slides or a still image do not need to be watched.
  • You want to listen on the move. Audio plays happily with the screen off and works well with headphones or a car stereo.
  • Storage or data is tight. An hour of audio might be around 60 MB, while the same hour as 720p video could be several hundred megabytes or more.
  • You are reviewing your own work. Creators often listen back to their own podcast or talk to improve delivery.

What you can save

Audio is protected by copyright just like video. Good examples of content you can usually save include:

  • your own podcast episodes, talks or performances that you posted as videos;
  • lectures or classes where the teacher or institution has said recordings may be downloaded for study;
  • talks or interviews released under a license that allows downloading, such as many Creative Commons licenses; and
  • recordings a friend, colleague or organiser has given you permission to keep.

Commercial music, audiobooks and paid courses are almost always protected, and being able to play them online does not mean you may download them. If you are not sure, ask the creator. Our guide to creator rights and Creative Commons explains how licenses work.

How to save audio with AllVid

  1. Copy the link to the video, or share it to the AllVid Android app.
  2. Paste it into AllVid.
  3. Choose an audio option, usually MP3 or M4A, instead of a video size.
  4. If you see a "getting ready" step, keep the page or app open while the audio is prepared. Converting long recordings can take a little while.
  5. Find the file in My files, where you can play, share or remove it.

MP3 or M4A?

Both are good. M4A often gives slightly better sound for its size and plays on all modern phones. MP3 plays on almost anything, including older car stereos and basic devices. For spoken word, either is fine. See MP3 vs M4A explained for the details.

Listening tips for long recordings

  • Use a podcast or audiobook-style player. Many audio apps can open local files and remember where you stopped, which is very useful for hour-long lectures.
  • Try playback speed. Listening at 1.25× or 1.5× can save time for talks and lectures, and many people find it easy to follow once they are used to it.
  • Use bookmarks or notes. Write down the time of important moments so you can jump back for revision.
  • Organise by series. Put lecture series or podcast seasons in their own folders with numbered names, such as "Biology 01 – Cells.m4a", so they stay in order.

For creators: getting your own audio back

If you record a podcast or talk as video and post it online, you might not have kept a separate audio file. Saving the audio from your own post gives you a quick copy for reposting to a podcast platform, editing a highlight clip or archiving. The best quality, though, will always come from your original recording. Platforms compress uploads, so if you still have the original file, use that for editing and publishing.

When you reuse audio in new projects, remember that any background music in your video may belong to someone else, even if you made the rest of the video yourself.

For students: study responsibly

Lecture recordings are a great revision tool. Check your school's or teacher's rules about downloading and keep recordings for your own study. Do not reshare them publicly, especially if they include other students' voices or questions, which raises privacy concerns as well as copyright ones.

Keeping your audio library tidy

Audio files are small, so it is easy to collect hundreds. Rename files clearly, group them into folders and delete what you have finished. Our guide to organising your Downloads folder has a simple system, and the same approach works for audio.

The bottom line

For talks, lessons and your own podcasts, audio-only saving is lighter, more flexible and often more practical than video. Just make sure the recording is yours or that you have permission to keep it.
