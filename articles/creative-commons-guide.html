A beginner's guide to creator rights and Creative Commons licenses

Published October 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Not legal advice. This is a general introduction for everyday users. License terms and copyright rules vary, and the official license text always takes priority. For specific situations, especially commercial ones, please consult a qualified professional.

By default, creators hold copyright in what they make, and other people need permission to copy or reuse it. Some creators, though, want their work to travel further. Creative Commons licenses give them a simple, standard way to say "you may use this, as long as you follow these conditions". Knowing how to read them helps you find content you really are allowed to save and share.

Creator rights in a nutshell

In most countries, copyright applies automatically when someone creates an original video, song, photo or piece of writing. The creator controls whether others can copy it, share it publicly or make new works based on it. Our article on copyright basics for everyday users covers this in more depth.

Permission can be given one person at a time, for example by replying "yes, you can keep it" to a message. A license is a way to give permission to everyone at once, under stated conditions.

What Creative Commons is

Creative Commons (CC) is a non-profit organisation that publishes a set of free, standard licenses. A creator who uses one keeps their copyright but tells the world in advance what others may do. Because the licenses are standard, you do not need to read a new contract each time; you just need to understand a few building blocks.

The building blocks

LettersNameWhat it means
BYAttributionYou must give credit to the creator. All CC licenses include this.
SAShareAlikeIf you adapt the work, you must share your new version under the same license.
NCNonCommercialYou may not use the work mainly for commercial purposes.
NDNoDerivativesYou may share the work as it is, but not share edited or adapted versions.

The six licenses

  • CC BY: the most open. Share and adapt for any purpose, with credit.
  • CC BY-SA: share and adapt, with credit, and share adaptations under the same license.
  • CC BY-NC: share and adapt for non-commercial purposes, with credit.
  • CC BY-NC-SA: non-commercial, with credit, and adaptations under the same license.
  • CC BY-ND: share unchanged copies for any purpose, with credit.
  • CC BY-NC-ND: the most restrictive. Share unchanged copies for non-commercial purposes, with credit.

There is also CC0, a tool creators can use to give up their rights as far as the law allows, placing work effectively in the public domain. Works whose copyright has expired are also in the public domain.

How to tell if a video is CC-licensed

  • Look in the video's description, about section or the creator's website for a statement such as "Licensed under CC BY 4.0".
  • Some platforms let creators choose a Creative Commons license when uploading, and show it on the video page.
  • Check that the person who applied the license actually owns the work. Someone re-uploading another person's video cannot give you a valid license for it.
  • Remember that a video may contain parts with different rights. Background music, clips or images inside a CC video might not be covered by the same license.

If there is no clear license, assume normal copyright applies and ask the creator for permission.

How to give proper credit

A widely used approach is "TASL": include the Title, Author, Source and License. For example:

"Morning in the Mountains" by Jane Doe, from her channel, licensed under CC BY 4.0.

Where possible, link to the original and to the license. If you changed the work, say so, for example "trimmed from the original".

Licenses do not override everything

  • Platform rules still apply. A site's terms may limit how you download content, even if the content itself is licensed openly.
  • Privacy still matters. A license covers copyright, not the privacy of people who appear in a video.
  • Follow every condition. If you break a condition, such as using an NC work commercially, the license may no longer protect you.

If you are a creator

Choosing a license is a personal decision. If you want people to share your work widely, a CC license can make that easy and clear. If you want to keep tight control, you do not need to use one at all. Either way, saying clearly in your description whether people may download your videos helps your audience do the right thing.

Putting it into practice

Before saving and reusing a video, ask: Is it mine? Has the creator given me permission? Or is it under a license whose conditions I can follow?
