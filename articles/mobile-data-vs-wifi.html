Mobile data vs Wi-Fi: saving data when downloading

Published October 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Downloading a video on mobile data feels instant and convenient, until you get a message saying you have used most of your monthly allowance. Video is by far the hungriest kind of data. Knowing roughly how much a download uses, and when to wait for Wi-Fi, can save money and frustration.

How much data does a video use?

Downloading a file uses about as much data as the file's size. The exact amount depends on length, resolution and how much movement is in the video, but these rough figures for one minute of typical video are a useful guide:

SizeData per minute (approx.)A 10-minute video
360p3–8 MB30–80 MB
720p10–25 MB100–250 MB
1080p20–50 MB or more200–500 MB or more
Audio onlyabout 1–2 MB10–20 MB

So a handful of 1080p videos can easily use a gigabyte or more. If your plan includes, say, 5 GB a month, that is a meaningful slice.

Wi-Fi vs mobile data at a glance

  • Wi-Fi at home or work usually has no small monthly cap and is often faster and more stable indoors. It is the best choice for large downloads and for saving many videos at once.
  • Mobile data is convenient everywhere but usually limited, can be slower in crowded places, and may cost extra when you go over your allowance or travel abroad.

Public Wi-Fi in cafés, hotels and airports can be useful too, though it is often slower and shared with many people. Stick to sites that use secure HTTPS connections, and avoid entering passwords or payment details on networks you do not trust.

Practical ways to save data

Wait for Wi-Fi for big files

If a video is longer than a few minutes or you want it in 1080p, it is usually worth waiting until you are on Wi-Fi. Make a habit of saving videos you want for a trip the evening before, at home.

Pick a smaller size on mobile data

When you must download on the go, choose 360p or 720p. On a phone screen the difference from 1080p is often small, while the data saving is large. AllVid's Settings let you set a preferred quality, so a modest default protects you from accidentally saving huge files. See 360p vs 720p vs 1080p for help choosing.

Save just the audio when that is enough

For talks, lessons or your own podcast episodes, audio only uses a tiny fraction of the data. See turning a video into audio.

Use your phone's data tools

  • Data usage tracking: both Android (Settings, Network and internet, or Connections, Data usage) and iPhone (Settings, Mobile Data or Cellular) show how much data each app has used. Set a warning so you know when you are close to your limit.
  • Data Saver or Low Data Mode: these limit background data use by apps. They do not stop a download you start yourself, but they reduce data used behind the scenes.
  • Per-app limits: on Android you can often block background data for specific apps, and on iPhone you can turn off mobile data for individual apps.
  • Auto-play settings: many social apps auto-play videos as you scroll, which uses data quickly. Look for a data saver or "auto-play on Wi-Fi only" option in each app's settings.

Things to watch out for

  • Roaming: data abroad can be very expensive unless your plan includes it. Check before you travel, and consider turning off data roaming.
  • Hotspots: if you share your phone's connection with a laptop, downloads on the laptop use your mobile allowance too.
  • Failed downloads: a download that stops halfway and is started again from scratch uses data twice. A stable connection avoids that. See why downloads stop.
  • Many downloads at once: starting several large files together on a weak connection can slow them all down. In AllVid's Settings you can choose how many downloads run at once.

Does AllVid use my data?

Yes, in the same way as any download: the file you save comes over your current connection, whether that is Wi-Fi or mobile data, and its size is roughly the amount of data used. Pasting a link and seeing the available sizes uses only a very small amount. Apart from loading the app itself, the only large files AllVid downloads are the ones you choose to save.

A simple rule of thumb

Short clips and audio on mobile data are usually fine. Long videos, high resolutions and batches of files are best left for Wi-Fi. Combine that with a sensible default quality and a data warning on your phone, and you are unlikely to get a surprise bill.
