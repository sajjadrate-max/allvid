Saving videos responsibly: copyright basics for everyday users

Published October 25, 2026 · 4 min read

Not legal advice. This article is a general introduction written for everyday users. Copyright law differs from country to country and depends on the details. If you have a specific question, especially for business use, please speak with a qualified lawyer.

Saving a video to your phone feels like a small thing, and often it is. But every video was made by someone, and the law gives that person rights over their work. Understanding the basics helps you enjoy and keep content without harming the people who create it.

What copyright is

Copyright is a set of legal rights that the creator of an original work automatically receives. It covers videos, music, photos, artwork, writing and more. In most countries, you do not need to register anything or add a © symbol; the protection exists from the moment the work is created and recorded.

Copyright typically gives the owner control over copying the work, sharing it with the public, and making new versions of it. Others generally need permission to do those things, unless a legal exception applies.

Who owns a video?

Usually, the person who filmed and made the video owns it. A few situations are worth knowing:

  • Your own uploads: if you filmed and edited it, it is yours. Backing up your own posts is the clearest case of responsible saving.
  • Videos you appear in: appearing in a video does not usually make you the owner. The person who filmed it typically holds the copyright, so ask them before saving.
  • Work made for an employer: videos created as part of a job often belong to the employer.
  • Music in videos: a video can contain several layers of rights. Even if a creator owns their footage, the song playing in the background may belong to a record label and songwriter.

Uploading does not mean giving it away

A common misunderstanding is that anything posted publicly is free to take. It is not. When creators post on a platform, they usually give that platform permission to show their work, but they keep their copyright. Being able to watch a video for free does not mean you may download, re-upload or sell it.

What permission looks like

Permission can come in a few forms:

  • Direct permission: the creator tells you that you may save their video. A quick message asking is often all it takes, and many creators are happy to say yes for personal use.
  • Licenses: some creators release work under licenses such as Creative Commons. These spell out what you may do, for example share with credit, or use only for non-commercial purposes. Check which license applies and follow its terms.
  • Public domain: very old works whose copyright has expired, and some works deliberately dedicated to the public, can be used freely.
  • Platform features: some apps offer a built-in download button that the creator can turn on or off. When it is available, the creator and platform have chosen to allow saving within that app's rules.

Exceptions, briefly

Many countries have exceptions that allow limited use without permission, such as "fair use" in the United States or "fair dealing" in the United Kingdom, Canada and elsewhere. These can cover things like commentary, criticism, news reporting, teaching or research. They are judged case by case and are narrower than many people think. Do not assume an exception covers you; when in doubt, ask for permission.

Platform rules matter too

Separate from copyright law, every site has terms of service that you agree to when you use it. Some limit downloading to the site's own features. Following those rules is part of using any downloading tool responsibly, and it is your responsibility as the user.

Good habits for everyday saving

  • Prioritise your own content. Backups of your own posts are always fine. See how to back up your own videos.
  • Ask first. If you love a creator's video and want to keep it, send a message. It is polite, and a yes removes any doubt.
  • Keep it personal. Even with permission to save, re-uploading or using someone's work commercially usually needs separate, explicit permission.
  • Share links, not files. When showing friends a video, sending the link gives the creator views and credit. See using the Share button on Android.
  • Give credit. When you are allowed to share someone's work, name them clearly.
  • Respect privacy. Never save or spread private videos of other people without their consent, even if you technically can.
  • Be extra careful with music and films. Commercial songs, TV shows and movies are almost always protected and rarely licensed for download from social sites.

The simple test

Before saving a video, ask yourself: "Is this mine, or has the person who made it said I can keep it?" If the answer is yes, go ahead. If you are not sure, ask, or simply bookmark the link instead. Respecting creators keeps them making the things we all enjoy.
